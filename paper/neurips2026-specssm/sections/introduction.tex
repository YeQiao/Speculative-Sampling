Autoregressive decoding in large language models (LLMs) is inherently sequential: each token depends on all previous tokens, making generation latency proportional to sequence length regardless of available compute.
Speculative decoding~\citep{leviathan2023fast,chen2023accelerating} addresses this by letting a small, fast \emph{drafter} model propose $K$ candidate tokens that the large \emph{verifier} checks in a single batched forward pass, accepting correct tokens and resampling at the first disagreement.

Two families of speculative drafters have emerged.
\emph{Internal} (self-drafting) methods reuse verifier components: Medusa~\citep{cai2024medusa} attaches parallel prediction heads, Draft \& Verify~\citep{zhang2024draft} exploits early-exit layers, and EAGLE~\citep{li2024eagle} autoregressively predicts the verifier's own feature vectors.
These approaches avoid training a separate model, but they share practical drawbacks: they consume GPU memory alongside the verifier, require architectural modifications or retraining, and provide diminishing returns when the verification pass itself dominates wall-clock time.
They are also tightly coupled to the verifier's internals and can degrade on out-of-distribution inputs.
\emph{External} pretrained drafters are independently trained and therefore more robust to distribution shift, but existing designs use transformer architectures that remain expensive in their own right.
Even a small transformer drafter like LLaMA-3.2-1B retains quadratic attention complexity and a KV-cache that grows linearly with context length.
At long contexts this cache competes for GPU memory with the verifier's own KV-cache, forcing painful trade-offs between drafter capacity and serving budget.
The problem is compounded in memory-constrained deployments: running a 70B verifier already consumes most of GPU VRAM, leaving little room for a billion-parameter drafter and its cache.

State space models (SSMs) offer a natural escape from both costs.
Mamba2~\citep{dao2024transformers} generates tokens in $O(1)$ time and memory per step with no KV-cache, making it an attractive drafter candidate.
\citet{choi2025mamba} confirmed that Mamba-based drafters achieve competitive acceptance rates at small scales.
However, two fundamental obstacles remain.
First, \emph{distribution misalignment}: a small recurrent SSM is architecturally far more distant from a large transformer verifier than a small transformer is, since it lacks attention entirely.
This gap makes alignment substantially harder, and vanilla SSM drafters underperform comparably sized transformer drafters without explicit guidance.
Second, \emph{hidden-state rollback}: unlike a transformer's KV-cache, which can be truncated to any prefix after speculative rejection, Mamba2's recurrent state folds the entire history into a fixed-size vector with no cheap rollback, requiring a full $O(T)$ re-prefill after every rejection round.
Beyond algorithmic challenges, deploying an SSM drafter is complicated by execution efficiency.
On GPU, Mamba2's sequential selective scan is difficult to parallelize for single-token generation, leaving the GPU underutilized during drafting.
On CPU, the recurrence is inherently sequential, and profiling reveals that linear projection layers account for 85\% of wall-clock time, creating a clear performance bottleneck that generic frameworks like PyTorch do not optimize for.

We address all four obstacles, misalignment, rollback, GPU underutilization, and CPU bottleneck, in a unified framework.
We present \textbf{SpecSSM}, which combines an externally pretrained Mamba2 drafter with three key innovations.
(1)~\emph{Guided injection}: lightweight linear projections inject verifier hidden-state signals into Mamba2's state-update (\texttt{x}-branch) pathway, maneuvering the recurrent drafter toward the verifier's distribution in real time inference at a cost of only 0.3\% additional parameters and with no modification to the verifier. We demonstrate that it bridges the larger architectural gap to recurrent models and is mandatory for SSM drafters to work effectively.
(2)~\emph{Activation replay}: we snapshot the SSM state before each draft round and replay only accepted tokens after rejection, reducing resynchronization from $O(T)$ full re-prefill to $O(K)$ replay (up to $201\times$ faster drafting at 1024-token contexts on CPU).
(3)~\emph{CPU offloading with a custom kernel}: because the drafter is tiny, we offload it entirely to CPU, freeing all GPU memory and computing resourcesfor the verifier.
We developed a custom C++ kernel stack using AVX-512 intrinsics and INT8 quantization targets the linear-projection bottleneck, achieving $7.5\times$ speedup over na\"ive PyTorch and completing 8-token drafts in 9\,ms, which fits inside the 16\,ms GPU verification window for asynchronous pipeline overlap.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{guided external SSM drafter} that injects verifier hidden states into Mamba2's state-update branch. With backbones of 27M and 45M parameters ($44\times$ and $26\times$ smaller than LLaMA-3.2-1B), guidance improves acceptance by 25--85\% over unguided baselines. The approach transfers across verifier families (LLaMA, Gemma) and scales with verifier size (TBD mean with LLaMA-70B, +35\% over 8B).
    \item \textbf{Activation replay}, an efficient SSM state resynchronization method that avoids full re-prefill after speculative rejection, providing up to $201\times$ speedup on CPU at 1024-token contexts.
    \item A \textbf{CPU-offloaded drafting pipeline} with a bottleneck-targeted AVX-512/INT8 kernel achieving $7.5\times$ over torch.compile, where draft latency (9\,ms) fits inside the GPU verification window (16\,ms) for asynchronous overlap, achieving $2.7\times$ throughput over autoregressive decoding.
\end{enumerate}
