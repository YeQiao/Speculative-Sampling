Autoregressive decoding in large language models (LLMs) is inherently sequential: each token depends on all previous tokens, making generation latency proportional to sequence length regardless of available compute.
For interactive applications such as chatbots, code assistants, and real-time translation, this latency directly impacts user experience.
Speculative decoding~\citep{leviathan2023fast,chen2023accelerating} offers an elegant solution: a small, fast \emph{drafter} model proposes $K$ candidate tokens, and the large \emph{verifier} checks them in a single forward pass, accepting a prefix of correct tokens and resampling from the first disagreement.

\paragraph{Self-drafting vs.\ external drafting.}
Two families of speculative drafters have emerged.
\emph{Self-drafting} methods reuse verifier components: Medusa~\citep{cai2024medusa} attaches parallel prediction heads, Draft \& Verify~\citep{zhang2024draft} exploits early-exit layers, and EAGLE~\citep{li2024eagle} autoregressively predicts the verifier's own feature vectors.
Because these approaches share the verifier's weights or hidden states, they avoid training a separate model, but they carry practical drawbacks.
They consume additional GPU memory alongside the verifier, they require architectural modifications or retraining of the verifier, and the latency savings they provide are fundamentally limited: when the verification pass itself dominates wall-clock time (the common regime for large verifiers), reducing draft cost to near zero yields diminishing returns.
Moreover, self-drafters are tightly coupled to the verifier's internals and degrade on out-of-distribution inputs where the verifier's intermediate representations are less predictive.

\emph{External pretrained drafters} are independently trained models that retain standalone generation capabilities.
Because they learn general next-token distributions from broad pretraining corpora, they achieve higher acceptance rates and are more robust to distribution shift.
The longstanding challenge is that transformer-based external drafters (e.g., LLaMA-3.2-1B at 1.2B parameters) are still expensive: they carry quadratic attention costs, large KV-cache memory, and latency that competes with the verifier.
Recent work by~\citet{berdoz2026steering} has shown that injecting verifier hidden states into pretrained transformer drafters via steering vectors can boost acceptance by up to 35\%, confirming that external drafters benefit from alignment signals.
Meanwhile,~\citet{choi2025mamba} demonstrated that Mamba-based SSMs are compelling drafter candidates thanks to linear-time generation and constant per-token memory, but their vanilla SSM drafter lacks any alignment to the verifier.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We resolve the external drafter's cost problem by going extremely small and offloading entirely to CPU.
We present \textbf{SpecSSM}, the first framework to combine an externally pretrained SSM drafter with verifier-guided injection and full CPU offloading.
Our drafter is a Mamba2 model with only 27M backbone parameters ($44\times$ smaller than LLaMA-3.2-1B).
Lightweight linear projections inject verifier hidden-state signals into Mamba2's state-update (\texttt{x}-branch) pathway, steering the recurrent drafter toward the verifier's distribution at a cost of only 0.3\% additional parameters, with no modification to the verifier.

A unique challenge for SSM drafters is \emph{cache resynchronization}: unlike transformers whose KV-cache can be truncated after rejection, Mamba2's recurrent hidden state has no efficient rollback mechanism.
We address this with \emph{activation replay}, which snapshots the pre-draft state and replays only accepted tokens through the recurrence after rejection.
This reduces resynchronization from $O(T)$ full re-prefill to $O(K)$ replay, a critical optimization for CPU-offloaded deployment where re-prefill costs 8 seconds at 1024-token contexts versus 40\,ms for replay.

To exploit the drafter's tiny footprint (27M backbone, $\sim$55\,MB in FP32 excluding the shared embedding), we offload it entirely to CPU, freeing all GPU memory for the verifier.
Our profiling reveals that linear projections account for 85\% of Mamba2 CPU inference time.
Targeting this bottleneck, we implement a custom C++ kernel stack using AVX-512 intrinsics and INT8 quantization that achieves $7.5\times$ speedup over na\"ive PyTorch CPU inference, completing 8-token draft rounds in 9\,ms.
Since LLaMA-8B verification takes 16\,ms per round, the CPU draft fits entirely within the GPU verification window, enabling asynchronous pipeline parallelism with a projected $2.7\times$ throughput over autoregressive decoding.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{guided external SSM drafter architecture} that injects verifier hidden states into Mamba2's state-update branch. With only a 27M backbone ($44\times$ smaller than LLaMA-3.2-1B), guidance improves mean acceptance by 25\% over the unguided pretrained baseline (2.33 vs.\ 1.86 with LLaMA-8B; 2.51 with LLaMA-70B, +35\% over the 8B verifier).
    \item \textbf{Activation replay}, an efficient SSM state resynchronization method that avoids full re-prefill after speculative rejection, providing up to $201\times$ speedup on CPU at 1024-token contexts.
    \item A \textbf{CPU-offloaded drafting pipeline} with a bottleneck-targeted AVX-512/INT8 kernel achieving $7.5\times$ over PyTorch, where draft latency (9\,ms) fits inside the GPU verification window (16\,ms) for asynchronous overlap.
    \item A \textbf{masking analysis} documenting a previously unreported interaction between non-compact speculative decoding layouts and recurrent drafters, where omitting rejection masks inflates SSM acceptance while deflating transformer acceptance.
\end{enumerate}
